1. The idea in one paragraph

Serious bowlers already pay a pro shop to measure their hand to a 64th of an inch and drill three holes into a $200 ball at specific spans and pitches. That fit exists in exactly one place: the ball. FitSlice prints the patch of ball surface the hand actually touches, with the bowler’s own drill sheet reproduced in it, so the release can be practiced anywhere: fingertip grip holds, thumb-exit timing reps and finger-lift drills on a 320-gram part instead of a 15-pound ball on a lane. A free web configurator turns the drill sheet into the model in about a tenth of a second; we print and ship, or sell the file. No product we could find does this: the closest things on the market are soft practice balls with generic one-size holes and wrist braces sized small/medium/large C4C6.

2. How it actually helps (the honest version)

You asked for this plainly, so here it is without marketing.

What creates revs for a thumb-in bowler. Coaches agree on the sequence: the thumb leaves the ball first and cleanly, and in the fraction of a second before the fingers leave, the fingers travel up and around the ball, driven by the wrist. The longer and faster that finger action, the more revolutions E7E8E9. Three things wreck it: a thumb that hangs in the hole too long, a hand that is squeezing, and fingers that are not in a position to lift E10E11E12.

Where fit comes in. Squeezing is mostly a fit problem. The pro-shop trade body’s own rule of thumb is that hole size is only about 20% of a fit; span and pitch are 40% each E13. Oversized holes make the hand squeeze, which limits hook; too much tape makes the thumb hang E12. That is why serious bowlers tier tape inside the thumb hole in 64ths of an inch E14: the geometry of three holes decides whether the hand can stay relaxed.

What the trainer does. It gives the bowler their exact geometry away from the lane, so three things can be practiced that are hard or impossible to practice with a real ball:

  1. Hanging the weight on relaxed fingertips. Holding the puck at your side with the thumb loose rehearses carrying weight on the finger pads with the thumb doing nothing, which is the habit that lets you stop squeezing on real shots. Be clear-eyed about load: a 300 g puck is about 2% of a 15-lb ball, so on its own it trains the feel and the habit, not strength. For strength, hang a weight from the wrist cord (1–3 kg) or fill the pocket; strength training transfers best to the exact position and task that was trained (a 2025 meta-analysis found roughly half the effect when strength is tested in a different mode than it was trained in E15), and sport-specific finger loading raises finger strength measurably in 4–8 weeks E16E17. A generic hand gripper is the wrong shape; the loaded trainer is the right shape by definition.
  2. Thumb-exit timing. Dangle the puck, relax the thumb and let it slide out while the fingers catch the weight. That is the exact “thumb leaves, fingers stay” moment of a high-rev release, rehearsed in isolation with instant feedback (if the puck falls, the fingers were not carrying it). You cannot safely drill this with a 15-pound ball in your living room.
  3. Finger lift. The fingers-only spinner disc is released onto carpet; it spins in place and the engraved bars let you count how long it spins. More lift, longer spin. It is a feedback device, not a measuring instrument.

What it does not do, and we must not say it does. No study shows that any off-lane trainer raises rev rate or average E18. More revs is not automatically better: coaches stress matching revs to speed, and a 270-RPM Hall of Famer out-struck crankers by getting the ball into a roll at the pins E19E20. The trainer does not teach footwork, timing or targeting, and it will not fix a bad fit; it copies whatever fit you give it. The claim we can stand behind today is narrower and still worth money: it is the only way to practice your real grip and release off the lane, and it trains the two things every coach says the release depends on, a relaxed hand and an early, clean thumb exit. Part 6 lays out which claims are safe.

Why a slice and not a ball. A regulation ball is 8.5 inches across and 12–16 pounds C12. Holes drilled at zero pitch aim at the ball’s center; that convergence is what makes thumb and fingers oppose each other around the curvature. The trainer keeps the real 108 mm radius and drills the holes toward the same center with the bowler’s pitches applied, so the hand sits exactly as it sits on the ball. It weighs about 300 g at 20% infill X6, which is light enough for long holds and heavy enough to need real fingertip pressure; a weight pocket in the base takes coins or washers for progressive loading.

3. Who buys it

Core customer: the certified league bowler with a fitted ball. There were 1,075,194 USBC members in 2024–25, roughly flat into 2025–26 after falling from 1.70 million in 2013–14 M1M2M3. These are the people who have a drill sheet to type in. They are 65% male M6; the tournament-traveling subset is 87% male and 70% aged 50 and over M7, which is squarely the one-handed, thumb-in generation. About 58,000 of them travel to the USBC Open Championships every year M8, so they spend on the sport.

The funnel above them is huge but casual. USBC says 67 million Americans bowl at least once a year M4; survey-based counts put it near 45 million M5; an older analysis found 93% of bowlers bowl four times a year or fewer and only about 21% are “serious” M9. Casuals do not have drill sheets. They are not customers for the custom product, though they are for a stock-size sizer plate and a “find your fit” story.

Youth is the hedge, not the core. USBC Youth has over 102,000 members and Junior Gold drew more than 3,500 competitors in 2025 M10M11, but the elite youth game has gone two-handed (no thumb): the only published estimate says upwards of 20% of youth bowl two-handed, and the 2025 Junior Gold U18 boys stepladder was all two-handed M12M13. The fingers-only spinner is the product for them; the puck is for the adult thumb-in majority.

Secondary markets. Japan is large but shrinking fast (16.9 million participants in 2011 to 9.3 million in 2019) M14; Korea’s centers are growing M15. Both are STL-download markets, not shipping markets, at first.

Realistic sizing. We did not find any survey of what a league bowler spends per year on equipment or coaching, so any such figure is ours: a new high-performance ball runs roughly $150–$250 plus $40–$150 to drill M16C11, and a bowler who buys two balls a year is spending $400–$800 before shoes, bags and league fees. If 1% of USBC members bought a $119 pair over three years, that is about 10,700 units and $1.3 million in revenue. That is a niche business, not a venture-scale one, and the plan is written for that: a profitable one-to-three-printer operation first.

Two facts from the fit world matter most for the pitch. First, bowlers already pay to clone one hole: custom silicone thumb molds cost $15 for a master and $20 per copy, and interchangeable-thumb systems (Turbo Switch Grip, JoPo Twist, Vise IT) reduce the thumb to a standard slug the pro shop custom-drills C11. Second, the pro-shop measuring tools that hold a bowler’s fit cost about CA$1,342 and live in the shop C12. The fit is valuable, portable as data (shops accept uploaded drill sheets C11) and has no at-home physical form. That is the gap.

Prior art to respect: a 2007 US patent on a foam “bowling practice device with finger holes for personalizing the fit” (US7261638) is still in force until roughly 2027 and needs a freedom-to-operate review before launch; the 1987 grip-exerciser patent and the thumb-exit-aid patents are expired C14. We are not aware of any active patent on generating per-bowler hole geometry from a drill sheet, which is the core of what we do.

Capacity is the real constraint: one printer yields about 25–30 pucks a month (roughly $2,500–3,000 of revenue at the single price, more with pairs); three printers running pucks and spinners is a real part-time business at about $8,000–10,000 a month of revenue, and the printers pay for themselves within their first month of sold output.

5.3 Operations

The pipeline already exists and is in the repository:

  1. Customer enters the drill sheet in the configurator (fractions accepted: 55/64, 1 1/32, 4-1/2; mm or inches; pitches in drill-sheet inches).
  2. The configurator rebuilds the model in ~100 ms, checks for bores that would collide, and produces the STL plus a fit-sheet JSON that is stored with the order.
  3. We print flat-side down in PETG, 4 walls, 20% infill, no supports. Bores get a wipe of fine sandpaper. Customer finishes fit with tape, exactly as on a ball.
  4. Fit guarantee: the sheet is on file; if the printed bores miss the sheet, we reprint. Returns on custom goods are otherwise limited, which is standard for personalized products.

Print tolerance is the one technical risk in the chain: printed holes come out 0.2–0.4 mm small, which is why the configurator adds a print allowance and why the Fit Kit sizer exists (it is printed on the same printer and material as the order, so the bore that fits is the bore we print). Selling the Fit Kit first cuts refit risk to near zero.

5.4 Product roadmap

Five configurator features are planned that address print tolerance, fit intake for bowlers without a drill sheet, printer calibration and strength loading. They are described in the provisional patent application (kept outside the public repository until it is filed) and are deliberately not detailed here, so that they keep their novelty in jurisdictions without a grace period. 6. Claims we can make

Think of this as the copy deck’s legal spine. Green claims are supportable today with the sources in Part 8. Yellow claims need the qualifier shown. Red claims are not available until we have run the pilot in Part 7.

Green — make freely, cite when challenged

  • “Built from your drill sheet: hole sizes in 1/64” steps, both spans, forward/reverse and lateral pitch for every hole, on a regulation 8.5” ball radius.” (Product fact about the model, verified against the CAD source to 0.1 mm. Say “exact” about the printed part only after the dimensional study in Part 7 shows printed bores land within a tape layer of nominal.)
  • “Holes are aimed at the ball’s center, the way a zero-pitch hole is drilled, so your thumb and fingers oppose each other exactly as on your ball.” (Product fact.)
  • “Coaches agree the thumb must leave first, and a relaxed hand creates more revs.” (Attributed coaching consensus E7E8E11.)
  • “The pro-shop trade body puts hole size at about 20% of a fit; span and pitch are 40% each. We copy all three.” (Attributed E13.)
  • “Oversized holes make you squeeze; squeezing limits hook.” (Attributed to coaching sources E12.)
  • “Weighs about 300 g, light enough for long holds, heavy enough to need real fingertip pressure.” (Product fact; state the infill.)
  • “Unlike generic one-size trainers and S/M/L wrist braces, it is built from your fit.” (Comparative, factual, with the examples in Part 4 available as substantiation.)

Yellow — make with the qualifier

  • “Designed for fingertip grip holds in the exact position you bowl in; add weight on the cord for strength work” (not “builds strength”: at 300 g the puck alone is a feel-and-habit tool); back the loaded version with the specificity literature E15E16 when asked, and never imply a measured result for bowlers.
  • “Practice a clean, early thumb exit” (not “fixes your thumb exit”).
  • “To our knowledge, the first trainer that uses your actual drill sheet” (we searched; absence of evidence is not proof).
  • “Short daily sessions help keep the feel between league nights” (spacing and skill-decay research supports frequent practice in general E21E22, but the effect is task-dependent E23; do not claim “science proves 10 minutes a day”).

Red — do not claim until the pilot supports it

  • Any number for rev-rate gain, hook gain or average gain (“adds 50 RPM”, “adds 10 pins”).
  • “Prevents injury” or “prevents bowler’s thumb.” (Bowler’s thumb is a documented fit-related injury E24, and a fit check is standard advice, but no study links a trainer to prevention.)
  • “Recommended by pro shops / coaches / USBC / PBA” until a named one has said so in writing.
  • Any use of a ball brand’s name or trade dress (“Storm-style”, “fits like a Hammer”).
  • Testimonials that quote results (“went from 300 to 380 RPM”) unless we can show they are typical or clearly disclose they are not; the FTC’s endorsement rules treat atypical results shown without disclosure as deceptive.

Mottos and taglines

Primary, for the product and the site: “Your drill sheet, off the ball.”

Supporting lines, each tied to a true property:

  • “Train the hand that bowls.” (It is the hand’s contribution, not the ball’s.)
  • “Same holes. Same pitch. No lane fee.”
  • “Relax the thumb. Let the fingers work.” (The coaching idea in six words.)
  • “Fit you can practice.”
  • “Revs start where the thumb leaves.”
  • For the Fit Kit: “Find your size before you print your fit.”
  • For the spinner: “Lift it, spin it, count it.”

Avoid anything that promises a number, uses “pro” as an endorsement, or borrows a brand’s vocabulary (no “Hook Monster”, no “Belmo-style”).

7. Earning the performance claims: the pilot

Everything the market wants to hear (“it adds revs”) is a red claim today, and even “exact fit” is a claim about printed parts that nobody has measured yet. Three cheap studies, in this order:

Study 1, before any marketing spend: does the printer reproduce the sheet? Print 30 pucks from one specification, measure every bore with pin gauges or a digital caliper (about $100 of tools), and report mean error and standard deviation per hole and per pitch direction. Target: standard deviation under 0.1 mm, with a per-printer compensation offset derived from the data. Drill sheets step in 1/64” (0.4 mm) and bowlers feel one layer of tape, so this is the number the whole product rests on. Also run a heat test (two hours at 65 °C with a weight on the part, then re-measure the bores; PETG softens near 80 °C and parts live in car trunks) and a 10,000-cycle insert/release test with a thumb-sized dowel. Cost under $500.

Study 2: can bowlers feel their own fit? For 15 bowlers, print the true specification plus two decoys (thumb hole 1/64” larger and 1/64” smaller) and ask each to pick theirs blind. Nine or more of fifteen correct beats chance (one in three). About 45 prints, roughly $450 of filament. If bowlers cannot pick their own, “exact fit” becomes “built from your drill sheet” and the insert-ready option (bores sized for the bowler’s own inserts and thumb slug, Part 5) moves up the roadmap.

Study 3: the performance pilot. 20–24 one-handed league bowlers, randomized to trainer (10 min/day, the six drills) plus league play, or league play only, for 6 weeks. Measures: rev rate from 240 fps phone video with a tape stripe on the ball (or two sessions per bowler at a center with Specto, about $10 each C8), counted blind to group; frames between thumb clear and finger clear from a side view (consistency of that interval is the more defensible outcome); a hand dynamometer ($30–60) before and after; league sheets for game-1 versus game-3 scores (first-game rust is the observable for “keeps the feel”); comfort ratings, reported as comfort, never as injury. Pre-register the measures and publish whatever comes out. Be honest about power: with n≈20 and roughly ±20 RPM measurement noise this detects a shift of about 30 RPM or more; it is a go/no-go signal, not proof. Cost: 24 pairs plus small gift cards, $1,500–3,000.

Until Study 1 passes, “exact” stays out of headlines; until Study 3 reports, performance is communicated as “practice what coaches say matters” and the 30-day fit guarantee carries the risk.

8. Selling it to people who do not know what it is

The problem with the product is that it needs a sentence of explanation before anyone wants it. The sales plan is built around removing that sentence.

The ten-second explanation. “It’s the part of your ball your hand touches, printed with your exact holes, so you can practice your release anywhere.” Use it everywhere, verbatim. Then show, do not tell: a 15-second clip of a thumb pop (puck drops onto the fingers) says more than a paragraph.

Let them type in their own numbers. The configurator is the hook. A bowler who enters their spans and pitches and watches their grip appear in 3D is already imagining holding it. The call to action after the model appears is “Print my fit” and, for the hesitant, “Start with the $24 Fit Kit.”

Lead with fit, not revs. Revs is a promise we cannot quantify; fit is a fact we can show. “Does it feel like your ball?” is a question we win every time, and it is the question a skeptic will test first.

Objections and answers.

  • “I can just hold my ball.” Your ball is 15 pounds and drilled to be thrown, not held for 30 seconds at your desk, and you cannot drop-catch it to drill thumb exit. The trainer is a third of a kilo and goes in a bag.
  • “Does it actually add revs?” Honest answer: it trains the two things every coach says the release depends on, a relaxed hand and an early thumb exit, with your real fit. Nobody has measured off-lane trainers, including us, which is why we are running a pilot and why there is a 30-day fit guarantee.
  • “My fit changes.” So does the file. Your sheet is stored; a reprint with the new sheet costs less than a re-drill.
  • “Why not a wrist brace?” A brace holds your wrist for you during the shot. This trains your hand to do it without one.
  • “I’m two-handed.” Then the spinner is your product; the puck is for thumb-in bowlers.

Channels, in order of expected return.

  1. Instruction YouTube. Bowling’s teaching creators are exactly our audience: Darren Tang (~265K subscribers; his top video, “4 Ways To Hook A Bowling Ball”, has over 4 million views), Brad and Kyle (~229K), and coach JR Raymond (~82K, already reviews products) H1H2H3. Send a trainer printed from the creator’s own drill sheet; the unboxing writes itself. Coach affiliate code at 15%.
  2. The configurator as content. Short videos of real drill sheets becoming models; a “paste your drill sheet” post in r/Bowling (57K–111K members) H4.
  3. Tournaments with vendor areas. The USBC Open Championships runs about 150 days with more than 58,000 adult bowlers passing through one building M8; Junior Gold’s trade show is described as drawing over 9,000 attendees H5; PBA and PWBA regionals are small, frequent and local H6. A booth with a sizer plate and a 3D printer running is a demo that explains itself.
  4. Pro shops as drill-sheet capture, not shelf space. No source gives a pro-shop count newer than 458 certified operators in 2017 M17 or any evidence that shops stock training aids H7. Shops matter because they hold the customer’s exact specs and the customer’s trust: a counter card and a 25% margin for entering the sheet costs nothing and makes the shop the hero.
  5. Marketplaces for discovery. Etsy’s 6.5% transaction fee covers personalization charges, plus $0.20 listing and 3% + $0.25 processing, with 12–15% on ad-attributed sales X3; its single free-text personalization box is a poor fit for a ten-field drill sheet, so Etsy listings link out to the configurator and the fit sheet comes back as the personalization text. Shopify’s variant system is the wrong tool regardless of its limit (the old 100-variant cap was raised to 2,048 in 2024–25; variants still cannot carry a ten-field drill sheet or generate geometry), and off-the-shelf parametric configurators cost $199/month and up H8, which is why the configurator is our own and writes the fit sheet into the order as line-item properties.

Launch sequence. Month 1: 20 seeded trainers to coaches and creators printed from their real sheets, feedback round, fix list. Month 2: Fit Kit and Pair on sale from the configurator; two creator videos live. Month 3: pilot study starts; first pro shop onboarded; Open Championships booth application. Month 6: pilot results published; pricing and claims updated.

9. Legal, IP and liability

Status: written from general knowledge, not from sources opened in this research pass. The legal researcher could not reach ftc.gov, ecfr.gov, uspto.gov, patents.google.com, bowl.com or the insurers’ sites, so nothing below is cited. Treat it as the agenda for a one-hour conversation with counsel, and pull the documents listed at the end before quoting any of it.

  • Advertising claims. US advertising law requires a reasonable basis for objective claims before they are made; for performance and health-adjacent claims the FTC expects competent and reliable evidence, which in practice means tests or studies, not coaching consensus. That is why Part 6 splits claims into green (product facts and attributed opinions), yellow (qualified) and red (performance numbers we do not have). Endorsements must reflect the endorser’s honest experience, results shown in testimonials must be typical or the typical result must be disclosed, and any material connection (a free trainer, an affiliate code, a sponsored video) must be disclosed clearly. A 2024 FTC rule on consumer reviews and testimonials carries civil penalties for fake or purchased reviews and for suppressing negative ones; the review program must be hands-off.
  • Governing body. The product is a training aid used off the lane. It is not a bowling ball, is not used in sanctioned competition, and to our knowledge no USBC approval applies to it. We say that plainly and never call it a ball, a “mini ball” or “regulation”.
  • Patents. Freedom-to-operate review of US7261638 (2007, “bowling practice device” with personalized finger holes) before launch; it should expire around 2027 but the claims need reading. The 1987 grip-exerciser patent and the thumb-exit-aid patents from the 1980s–90s are expired C14. Our own method (per-bowler hole geometry generated from a drill sheet, bores aimed at the regulation ball center with drill-sheet pitches, on a printed ball-radius slice) is documented with dated repository history; whether to file a provisional is a cost question for counsel.
  • Trademark. “Rev trainer” is descriptive and almost certainly unregistrable; the brand name is what we protect. Clear it against the USPTO register and common-law use before printing packaging. Never use a ball manufacturer’s name or trade dress in copy, photos or comparisons beyond plain factual references.
  • Product safety and liability. A hand-held sporting good for adults is generally not subject to a specific federal safety rule, but a product-liability claim needs no rule, so: operate through an LLC, carry general liability with product coverage (small e-commerce sellers typically see quotes in the low hundreds to about a thousand dollars a year; get real quotes), print the use warning on the card and the site (swing-and-hold and carpet-release drills only; never throw it; not for children under 12 without supervision), and ship PETG rather than PLA (tougher at the thumb bore). Check whether the chosen filament needs a California Proposition 65 notice.
  • Data. A fit sheet is personal data tied to a hand; store it with the order, state the retention period, and delete on request.

Documents to pull before launch: FTC Endorsement Guides, 16 CFR Part 255 (2023 revision) and the FTC’s “Endorsement Guides: What People Are Asking”; FTC Policy Statement Regarding Advertising Substantiation; FTC “Advertising FAQs: A Guide for Small Business”; the FTC Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials (2024); USBC Playing Rules on ball specifications and the equipment specifications page, to confirm nothing applies; Google Patents results for “bowling release trainer”, “bowling grip trainer”, “bowling practice device finger holes” with legal status; a USPTO register search for the brand name and for REV / REV TRAINER; CPSC guidance on General Certificates of Conformity for non-children’s products; SBA guidance on LLC formation in your state; two insurer quotes for general plus product liability.

10. Risks, plainly

  1. It is a small market. Roughly a million serious thumb-in candidates in the US, shrinking slowly for a decade M2; the growth segment (youth) is going two-handed M12M13. The business is sized for that: low fixed cost, printers that pay back in weeks, digital files for the rest of the world.
  2. The benefit is unproven. Every coach agrees on the mechanism and nobody has measured a trainer. The pilot is the answer; honest copy is the hedge.
  3. Fit transcription errors. A bowler mistypes a pitch sign and gets a trainer that feels wrong. Mitigations: the configurator shows the hole axes and warns on collisions, the Fit Kit calibrates size on the actual printer, pro shops can enter the sheet, and reprints are cheap.
  4. Copying. The design is simple to copy once seen. The moat is the configurator’s fit logic, the fit-sheet database and the relationships with coaches, not the shape.
  5. Data quality in this plan. Figures were gathered from search excerpts; a few (the 9,000-attendee trade show figure, the two-handed share, the online retail size) are single-source or estimates and are marked as such in Part 11.
  6. “Exact fit” is a manufacturing claim we have not measured. The model is exact; the printed bore is whatever the printer makes of it, and bowlers feel one layer of tape. Study 1 in Part 7 is the gate before marketing spend. The insert-ready option (bores that take the bowler’s own inserts and thumb slug) is the design answer if printed bores cannot hold the tolerance.
  7. Can the customer produce a drill sheet? Many league bowlers never see theirs; the pro shop has it. If most buyers cannot type in a sheet, the pro shop is a gate, not just a channel, and the “copy my ball” service (ship or photograph the ball) becomes the main intake. Cheapest test: a 300-response poll in r/Bowling and the large Facebook groups.
  8. Heat and durability. PETG softens near 80 °C and sporting goods live in car trunks; a warped bore destroys the only feature that matters. Test it (Part 7), say so on the card, and offer ASA or polycarbonate blends for hot climates.
  9. The name sells revs; the evidence sells fit. “Rev trainer” promises the one thing we cannot yet show. Lead the brand with fit and release practice; treat rev gains as the pilot’s hypothesis.
